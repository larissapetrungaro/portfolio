LM Cursos de Trânsito · New cart and checkout

I redesigned cart and checkout into a single page, with no password login required before paying, and created tiered discounts and cashback so students buy more than one course.

Context and problem

LM Cursos de Trânsito sells online courses for professional drivers: school transport, emergency vehicles, hazardous cargo (MOPP), safety standards (NRs) and more. On the new platform, the checkout’s goal was to increase conversion and repeat purchases. To do that, the product gained two levers that raise average order value: tiered discounts and cashback.

In the old checkout, the purchase was split into 3 steps (your details, payment and summary), and payment was only unlocked after logging in with a tax ID (CPF) and password. Anyone without an account, or who forgot their password, stopped there. The page also gave no reason to buy more than one course, and the top took up the whole first fold with a decorative image.

My role

What I ownedThe full cart, checkout and post-payment flow: architecture, rules for each state, UX copy, a clickable prototype with two sign-up paths, and light and dark themes.
What I did with the teamDiscount and cashback rules with the business, and the Google integration and account lookup with the developer, who is implementing it in LM’s system. I was the only designer on the project.

Key decisions

Decision 1

Show the tiered discount where the decision happens: in the cart

What I sawTo raise average order value, the quantity discount needed to be seen before payment, not discovered afterwards.
Options consideredA discount line only in the summary, a generic banner, or a progress bar with the exact amount left and related suggestions.
What I chose and whyA 5% → 10% → 15% bar with “Add 1 more course and save R$ 33.75” and contextual suggestions (“Goes well with emergency vehicles”). The empty cart points to the catalog to recover the purchase.
Decision 2

Frictionless sign-up without exposing data

What I sawSign-up is the most common drop-off point. On top of that, showing data from existing accounts would let anyone probe the user base by typing other people’s emails.
Options consideredMandatory login before checkout, a multi-step sign-up, or sign-up built into the checkout itself.
What I chose and whyTwo paths in the same block. With Google (One Tap or button), the account is created instantly. With the form, all 4 fields come at once and only the email checks the database. Recognition shows no data. Once everything is filled in, the block turns into a summary on its own, with no confirm button competing with the place-order button.
Decision 3

A post-payment that keeps the sale and drives the next purchase

What I sawA declined payment usually turns into abandonment, and an approved purchase was the end of the relationship, not the start of the next one.
Options consideredGeneric error and success messages, or specific states, each with a clear action.
What I chose and whyApproved: a single action and cashback highlighted for the next purchase. Declined: it starts by saying nothing was charged, offers 3 paths and keeps the order reserved. Processing: the button is disabled to prevent duplicate charges.

Solution

Payment methodsPix (5% off, preselected), credit card, Google Pay, NuPay and boleto (bank slip). Pix and boleto have their own instruction screens: the title talks about paying, not confirming.

Outcome

The new checkout is in development. The project took 2 months and 20 days. Once it’s live, these are the metrics that will show whether the two levers worked:

[Conversion]from cart to approved payment
[Average order value]courses per order, the effect of tiered discounts
[Repeat purchases]orders that use cashback within its 1-year validity

Learnings

What I’d take to the next projectEvery field and every step in checkout is a reason to give up. Removing the mandatory login was the most important change.
